## What to find out

- The problem in their words, and one specific person who has it, ideally someone they know by name.
- The last time that person ran into it: what happened, and what it cost them in time, money or worry.
- What they do about it today, and what's wrong with that. Where they do nothing, why it hasn't been worth doing.
- Why now: what changed that makes it possible or needed.
- Why them: what they know, who they know or what they can do that others can't.
- How they'd reach the first few people who have it.
- What would show those people want it: a commitment (money, time, an introduction), never a compliment.
- Who would pay, and roughly how.

## What people usually miss

- Building before talking to the people who have the problem.
- Describing a solution as if it were the problem ("people need an app for…").
- Everyone as the audience: the first users are a small, specific group.
- Asking friends whether they like the idea, and counting their yes.
- An idea many have tried that looks easy, with a reason it keeps failing that isn't visible from outside.

## How to ask

- About the past and real people, never "would you use…".
- One thing at a time for someone new, with an example of a useful answer.
- When they don't know something about the people who have the problem, that's the first thing to find out, not a reason to stop: say how they could find out.
- Once the problem and the people are clear, offer to read what people say about it online, and say what that would add.

## What to propose

A diagnosis around learning, not a product:

- A learning goal: finding out whether the people who have the problem want it solved enough to act.
- The problems as they understand them, each Assumed until people's own words back it.
- Under each, the cheapest check: talk to a handful of people who had the problem lately, ask for a commitment (a pre-order, a deposit, their time), or do the service by hand for a few people before building anything.
- Which check to run first, and why that one.
